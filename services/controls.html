Controls & automation

A building that tells the truth. Sequences, trending, fault detection, integration — the difference between a plant and a rumor.

What it is

Controls are the intelligence the hardware is waiting for. We write sequences of operation that a technician can read, then we implement them in Niagara, in the native BAS the owner already owns, or in a hybrid we will not let become a mess. Trending is not a luxury; it is how we know a valve is lying. Fault detection is a short list of things that matter — not a thousand alarms no one will acknowledge.

Integration when it is useful: fire alarm for smoke control, lighting for occupancy, hotel locks for setback, electrical meters for LL97. Integration as a hobby is how BAS projects die.

When a building needs it

When the plant is new and the sequence is still the factory default. When two systems heat and cool the same floor. When the only person who understood the graphics retired. When you are about to spend capital on tons you might not need if the reset actually ran.

How we work

We read the existing sequence — if there is one. We sit with engineering and name the failures they already know. Then we write. Points list, graphics that look like the building, alarms with priority. We commission by trend, not by a screenshot on start-up day. We train. We hand over credentials the owner owns.

We will not hold a building hostage to a proprietary dead end if we can help it. Open protocols, documented. If the owner wants a specific vendor, we will say whether we can make it behave.

What you get

  • Sequences of operation in English and in the controller
  • Graphics, trends, and a sane alarm philosophy
  • Fault detection on the plant and on critical spaces
  • Metering aligned to LL97 where needed
  • Owner-held credentials and a backup of the database
  • Training that is not a lunch

New York

Smoke control sequences that FDNY will test. Hospital alarming that nursing can use. After-hours downloads so we are not taking a trading floor down at noon. Cyber: we do not put a plant on the open internet because a vendor thought it was convenient.
