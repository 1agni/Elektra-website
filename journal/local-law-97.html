Local Law 97 is a design problem

A limit is not a scare. It is a number you design to, the way you design to NC-35 or to a floor-to-floor.

Every few months a building owner asks us, in a voice that has been coached by a webinar, what they should “do about Local Law 97.” The question is sincere. The framing is wrong. LL97 is not a campaign. It is a limit on greenhouse gas intensity for buildings over 25,000 square feet, with penalties that get less theoretical in 2030. A limit is a design problem. Acoustics are a design problem. So is a beam that cannot take a cooling tower. We do not hold seminars about beams. We size the dunnage.

The scare version of the law produces two failure modes. The first is paralysis: wait for a new incentive, a new interpretation, a new mayor. The second is shopping: a product that promises compliance the way a filter promises wellness. Both are expensive. Both leave the plant as it is, which is usually the thing that is actually emitting.

Start with the occupancy you have

The law cares about the building’s emissions limit by occupancy group, then about how you meet it. Owners still send us models that use the occupancy they wish they had. A tower with a data hall in the cellar and a trading floor that never sleeps is not a 9-to-5 office, no matter what the brochure says. If the limit is wrong, the pathway is theater.

We pull interval data. We walk the plant. We ask the chief engineer what runs at 2am. Then we name the EUI and the emissions, in the units the filing will use, not in a slide. Local Law 87 audits are useful here if they were done by someone who looked at the plant. Many were not. We treat a bad audit as a document to redo, not as a relic to honor.

The plant is usually the lever

Lighting (Local Law 88) matters. Envelope matters, when you can touch it. Occupant behavior matters, slightly. In a New York commercial building the mechanical plant is still where the carbon lives: steam, oversized chillers, constant-speed towers, simultaneous heat and cool, a sequence last written when oil was a personality. Right-sizing a plant that was drawn for a full floor plate that never leased is not “value engineering.” It is honesty. A 1,640-ton peak does not need 2,400 tons of vanity.

Heat recovery is the unglamorous hero. Buildings reject heat to the sky while boiling steam for domestic water. A heat-recovery chiller is not fashionable. It is arithmetic. Electrification is the fashionable sentence. Sometimes it is also the right one — if the service can take it, if the steam is actually leaving, if the peak can be managed without a feeder that Con Edison will not have until 2031. Sometimes the right move is to make the existing plant less stupid and to file a pathway that buys time for the feeder. We will say which. We will not sell a heat pump into a switchgear that will not accept it.

Offsets are not a design

There is a version of “compliance” that is a purchase. The law allows certain flexibility. We are not the moral police of an asset manager’s spreadsheet. We are the people who will tell you that buying your way out of a plant you have not measured is how you pay twice. Design first. Operate second. Purchase last, if at all, and with a number attached to a remaining gap, not to a feeling.

Phasing is the city

New York does not give you a dark building. Crane windows are Sundays. Shutdowns are overnight and political. A pathway that requires the whole plant to vanish in July is not a pathway; it is a wish. We phase: temporary tons, a machine at a time, a sequence that works in the in-between. The carbon filing should match the construction schedule, or the filing is a fiction the examiner has not caught yet.

Union labor, DOB alterations, FDNY if fuel moves, LPC if the roof is visible. These are not “stakeholders.” They are the job. A design that cannot be filed cannot be built. A design that cannot be built cannot reduce emissions. This is obvious and routinely forgotten by people who present in hotels.

What we will not do

We will not put a green banner on a website and call it a practice. (We use sage, once, when we are actually talking about energy.) We will not guarantee a penalty of zero independent of occupancy and operations. We will not model a fantasy envelope. We will not ignore the chief engineer. We will not treat 2030 as a surprise.

If you want a studio that will sit with the limit, measure the building, and draw a plant that meets it — that is the work. It is slower than a webinar. It is cheaper than a penalty you could have designed out. It looks, from the street, like nothing. That is the point of climate, made architectural.
